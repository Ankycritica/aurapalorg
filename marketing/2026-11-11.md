## Day 1 (Thu) — Reddit r/careerguidance (or r/jobs)

**Title:** The counter-offer email I'd send if I got an offer tomorrow (template + what to change)

A lot of people accept the first number because negotiating feels confrontational. Over email it doesn't have to be. Here's the shape I'd use:

> Hi [Name],
>
> Thank you for the offer. I'm excited about the role and the team, and I'd like to make this work.
>
> Before I accept, I'd like to discuss the base salary. Based on advertised ranges for [Job Title] roles at a similar level in [City], and the scope we discussed around [one specific responsibility], I was expecting something closer to [Number].
>
> Is there flexibility to move the base to [Number]? If so, I'm ready to move forward.

Why it's built this way:

- **Thanks + interest first.** Recruiters push harder internally for people they think will say yes.
- **One specific number, not a range.** Say "80 to 90" and you've agreed to 80.
- **The reason is the market and the role**, not your rent. Your costs are real, they just aren't why the job is worth more.
- **Name something the hiring manager actually said** in the interview. It ties your number to the job.
- **End with a question** so it invites a reply, not a standoff.

Then wait. Don't send a second email lowering your number before they answer.

The whole thing depends on [Number] being defensible — job postings with disclosed ranges for your role and city are the best public source.

I wrote up five more (competing offer, benefits when salary is fixed, asking your manager for a raise, following up after "not now", and accepting in writing): https://www.aurapal.org/blog/salary-negotiation-email-template

## Day 3 (Sat) — LinkedIn

Most salary negotiation now happens over email.

That's good news. You get time to think, there's no awkward silence on a call, and you end up with a written record of what was agreed.

The catch: it's easy to sound apologetic or aggressive by accident.

Five rules I'd apply to any negotiation email:

1. Thank them in one sentence. Gratitude isn't weakness.
2. Say you want the job (or want to stay).
3. Name one number. Not a range, not "a bit more".
4. Give one reason, based on the market or the scope of the role.
5. End with a question: "Is there flexibility here?"

And three things that quietly cost people money:
→ Accepting on the call instead of asking for a day to review
→ Opening with "sorry to ask"
→ Lowering your own number before they've replied

If you're asking for a raise rather than countering an offer: send the meeting request a couple of months before your company's budget cycle. Once the budget is set, even a supportive manager has little room.

"Thank you for the offer, I'd like to make this work. Based on advertised ranges for [role] in [city], I was expecting closer to [number]. Is there flexibility to move the base to [number]?"

3/ Salary band is fixed? Ask for something else: signing bonus, extra leave, a later start date, or a salary review at 6 months instead of 12. Pick two, not eight.

## Day 7 (Wed) — Reddit r/personalfinance or r/financialindependence (check rules; text-only, link only if allowed)

**Title:** If you're planning to ask for a raise this year, the timing matters more than the pitch

Internal raises are usually set from a budget decided well before review season. By the time your review happens, your manager is often allocating a fixed pot, and a great case can still get "the budget's already set."

What works better:

- Ask for a separate compensation conversation **a couple of months before the review/budget cycle**, not during it.
- Bring a market range for your role and city (job postings with disclosed salaries are the best public source; single-number averages flatten too much).
- Talk about **scope** — what you own now that you didn't a year ago — not hours or effort.
- Name one number.
- If the answer is "not now", ask for the bar and a date in writing. "Not now" with nothing attached, twice, tells you something.

Internal raises tend to track a percentage of your current salary, while outside offers track the market — so the longer you go without a re-level, the further you can drift below what the same company would pay a new hire.
