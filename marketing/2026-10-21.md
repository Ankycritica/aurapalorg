## Day 1 (Thu) — Reddit r/resumes

**Title:** Your resume summary should be a trailer for the page, not a list of adjectives. A 3-line structure + examples by career stage

Most summaries read like this: "Hard-working, detail-oriented professional passionate about delivering results." That could be anyone, so recruiters skip it.

A summary that earns its space does three things, one line each:

1. **Who you are, in the employer's words** — target title and level
2. **Your strongest proof** — one or two results, with numbers if you have them
3. **What you bring to this role** — the 2–3 skills from the job post you can back up below

Example (early career): "Customer success manager with four years in B2B SaaS, managing a book of 40 mid-market accounts. Cut churn on my book by a third in one year by introducing quarterly business reviews. Strong in onboarding design, renewals and Salesforce."

Example (career change): "Former high school teacher moving into instructional design. Designed a full year of curriculum for 150 students a year and built the department's online course materials. Completed a certificate in learning experience design."

Two rules that fix most summaries:

- Write it **last**, after your bullets. It's much easier once you know what your best results are.
- Every claim needs a bullet below that proves it. If the summary says "leadership", the reader will look for it.

I wrote up 12 examples (students, early career, senior, career changers) and the five mistakes that make summaries forgettable: https://www.aurapal.org/blog/resume-summary-examples

Post your summary in the comments and I'll suggest a rewrite.

## Day 3 (Sat) — LinkedIn

The resume summary is the first thing recruiters read and the last thing most people write.

That's actually the right order. Here's why.

A summary is a trailer. You can't cut a good trailer until you know what the best scenes are.

So:
→ Finish your experience bullets first
→ Pick the one result you'd most want an interviewer to ask about
→ Then write three lines: who you are, that result, and the 2–3 skills the job post asks for that you can prove

What to cut:
✗ "Results-driven", "passionate", "team player"
✗ "I am" / "I have" (implied first person saves space)
✗ Anything past four lines

What to keep:
✓ Your target title, in the employer's language
✓ One number
✓ Skills the job post mentions that you can back up on the same page

## Day 7 (Wed) — Reddit r/careerguidance (career changers angle)

**Title:** If you're changing careers, your resume summary has to translate for the recruiter. Here's how I'd write it.

When your job titles don't match the role you're applying for, the recruiter's first question is "why is this person here?" The summary is the only place to answer it before they move on.

What works:

1. **Lead with the transition, briefly.** "Retail store manager transitioning into operations management."
2. **Translate your experience into the new field's words.** Staff rota → scheduling. Stock counts → inventory control. Store budget → P&L ownership.
3. **Point at evidence.** "Ran a store with 25 staff, owned the budget and rota, and improved stock accuracy through weekly cycle counts."
4. **Show you're closing the gap.** A course, certificate or side project in the new field.

What doesn't work: an "objective" that only says what *you* want ("seeking a challenging role in operations"). Recruiters hire for their need, so lead with what you offer.

More examples for teachers, journalists and others moving fields: https://www.aurapal.org/blog/resume-summary-examples

Happy to help word yours if you share your old and target roles.
