## Day 1 (Thu) — Reddit r/interviews (or r/jobs)

**Title:** "Tell me about yourself" isn't small talk — it's a 60-second pitch. Use a present → past → future structure

Most people answer this question one of two ways: a life story that starts at school, or reading out the resume the interviewer is already holding. Both waste the one answer you get to fully control.

Behind the question are three narrower ones:

1. Who are you, professionally? (title, level, what you own)
2. What's the best evidence you're good at it?
3. Why are you here, and why now?

A structure that covers all three in about a minute:

- **Present** — what you do now, in one or two sentences
- **Past** — one or two experiences that explain how you got good at it (pick the most relevant, not the most recent)
- **Future** — why this specific role is the logical next step

Starting with the present stops you drifting into a chronological history. Ending on the future means the interviewer never has to guess why you applied.

The quiet trick: mention **one concrete result**. Interviewers usually ask about it next, which means your second question is one you've already prepared.

Mistakes I see constantly: "I work in tech" (say your title), no reason for this role, going past two minutes, and memorising a word-for-word script that collapses when you're interrupted.

I wrote it up with three full example answers (new grad, experienced hire, career changer): https://www.aurapal.org/blog/tell-me-about-yourself-answer

## Day 3 (Sat) — LinkedIn

The first interview question is the one most people prepare least.

"So, tell me about yourself."

It sounds like small talk. It isn't. It sets the frame for everything that follows.

What works is three short parts, about a minute in total:

→ Present: what you do now and what you own
→ Past: one or two experiences that explain how you got good at it
→ Future: why this role is the obvious next step

Two things make the difference:

1. Tailor the past and future parts to each job description. Your present stays the same; the rest shouldn't.
2. Include one specific result. It invites the follow-up question — and you'll already have the story ready.

For career changers, the past section does the heavy lifting: translate your old work into the new field's vocabulary so the interviewer doesn't have to. "Lesson planning" becomes "curriculum design".

## Day 7 (Wed) — Reddit r/careerguidance (career-changer angle)

**Title:** Career changers: your "tell me about yourself" answer has to translate your old job for the interviewer

If you're switching fields, the interviewer is quietly asking "why is a teacher / retail manager / journalist applying for this?" Your opening answer is the best place to answer that before they ask.

What worked in the examples I put together:

- **Present:** say what you did, plainly. "For six years I've been a secondary school science teacher."
- **Past:** pick the part of that job that overlaps the new one and name it in the new field's language. Teachers design curriculum; retail managers run budgets, rotas and stock control; journalists interview experts and explain complex topics under deadline.
- **Show you've already started.** A certificate, a freelance project, a volunteer build — something that proves the move is deliberate, not a whim.
- **Future:** tie it to something specific in their job post, not "I'm looking for growth".

Full example answer for a teacher moving into instructional design (plus a new-grad and experienced-hire version): https://www.aurapal.org/blog/tell-me-about-yourself-answer
