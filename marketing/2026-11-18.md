## Day 1 (Thu) — Reddit r/careerguidance (or r/careeradvice)

**Title:** If you're changing careers, stop sending your old resume with a new objective on top

This is the most common mistake I see from career changers, and it's an easy fix.

A recruiter reading a teacher's resume for a data analyst role spends a few seconds, sees "Teacher" and "taught Year 10 science", and moves on. The experience might be relevant. The document doesn't show it.

What works better:

1. **Pull 3–5 postings for the job you want** and list the skills and terms every one of them repeats. Those are the real requirements.
2. **Go through your history looking for overlap in what you did, not what your title was.** A teacher who built the department's grade tracker in Excel has done basic data work. A restaurant manager who built the staff rota has done operations.
3. **Use a hybrid format**: target title, 3-line summary that names the switch, a short skills section in the new field's vocabulary, then your experience in normal date order. Skip the purely "functional" format — recruiters know why people use it, and some ATS parse it badly.
4. **Rewrite every bullet for the new reader.** Same facts, different emphasis:
   - Before: "Prepared monthly management accounts."
   - After: "Built monthly reporting packs for the leadership team and answered ad-hoc questions about margin by product line."
5. **Add 2–3 projects** in the new field, even unpaid. That's the strongest evidence you can have.

And keep the dates. A gap or an odd timeline is easier to explain than missing dates.

Full write-up with more before/after examples and a sample summary: https://www.aurapal.org/blog/career-change-resume

## Day 3 (Sat) — LinkedIn

Career changers: your resume is probably still written for your old industry.

Not because the experience isn't relevant. Because the bullets assume context the new reader doesn't have, and highlight things they don't care about.

Three before/after rewrites:

Teacher → instructional designer
Before: "Taught Year 10 science to classes of 30."
After: "Designed and delivered a 12-week science curriculum, rewriting units based on assessment results."

Hospitality → operations
Before: "Managed restaurant staff and daily service."
After: "Ran daily operations with a team of 14, and built the rota and stock process the restaurant still uses."

Accountant → product analyst
Before: "Prepared monthly management accounts."
After: "Built monthly reporting for leadership and answered ad-hoc questions about margin by product line."

The facts didn't change. The emphasis did.

One rule: only keep numbers you can back up in an interview. If you don't know the exact figure, describe the scope instead.

## Day 5 (Mon) — X thread

1/ Changing careers without the job title you're applying for? Your resume has one extra job: explain in a few seconds why you're still worth a call. Here's how. 🧵

2/ Start from the target job, not your history. Pull 3–5 postings and list what they all repeat. That's the real requirements list.

3/ Then find the overlap in what you *did*, not your title. Built a tracker everyone adopted? That's data work. Cut staff turnover? That's hiring and retention.

4/ Use a hybrid format: target title, a 3-line summary that names the switch, skills in the new field's words, then experience in date order. Pure "functional" resumes make recruiters suspicious.

5/ Rewrite bullets for the new reader. "Prepared monthly accounts" → "Built monthly reporting for leadership and answered questions about margin by product line." Same facts, new emphasis.

6/ Add 2–3 real projects in the new field. They beat a list of certificates.
